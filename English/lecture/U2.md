A. What do you know about SpaceX, its reusable rockets and launching astronauts from the US? Discuss.

SpaceX has fundamentally changed the economics and cadence of space access by proving that orbital-class rockets can be recovered and reflown. The company’s Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy families are the first and only orbital rockets capable of reflight, landing either on autonomous droneships at sea or on landing zones near the launch pads. This reusability is presented as the pivotal breakthrough for substantially reducing launch costs: while the majority of a rocket’s cost comes from building a vehicle that historically flew only once, a rapidly reusable launch vehicle can, by analogy with commercial airliners, reduce the cost of traveling to space by a hundredfold. SpaceX also distinguishes itself by designing its rockets not merely to burn up on reentry but to withstand reentry, land back on Earth, and fly again.

On the human spaceflight side, SpaceX became the first private company to take humans to the International Space Station, in 2020. This restored the United States’ ability to launch astronauts from American soil—a capability that had been absent after the Space Shuttle program ended—and it did so through a commercial model rather than a traditional government-owned vehicle. The company’s launch infrastructure supports this dual role: Cape Canaveral Space Force Station provides access to low and medium inclination orbits used by communications satellites, Earth-observing satellites, and ISS supply missions, as well as to geostationary orbits and lunar or interplanetary departures; Kennedy Space Center’s Launch Complex 39A, the historic home of Apollo and the Space Shuttle, supports both commercial resupply missions and crewed Dragon launches; Vandenberg supports high-inclination and polar orbits for satellite constellations, defense intelligence, and Earth observation; and Starbase is being developed as the first commercial launch site designed for orbital missions and for Starship build and test.

Taken together, SpaceX’s reusability work and its crewed launches represent a shift from expendable, government-led access to space toward a commercially driven, rapidly reusable, and more cost-accessible model—one that the company explicitly frames as a step toward making humanity multiplanetary.

1. What have you learned about SpaceX’s achievements?
> SpaceX has achieved a series of historic firsts: it is the only private company capable of returning a spacecraft from low Earth orbit; in 2012 its Dragon spacecraft became the first commercial spacecraft to deliver cargo to and from the International Space Station; and in 2020 it became the first private company to take humans to the ISS. 2. What is the main advantage of reusable rockets?
>The main advantage is a substantial reduction in the cost of space access. Because the majority of launch cost comes from building the rocket—historically a vehicle that flew only once—a rapidly reusable rocket can spread the construction cost across many flights. SpaceX compares this to commercial aviation: a new airliner costs about the same as a Falcon 9 but can fly multiple times per day and conduct tens of thousands of flights over its lifetime. Following that model, a rapidly reusable space launch vehicle could reduce the cost of traveling to space by a hundredfold.
3. Why does SpaceX use several launch sites?
>SpaceX uses multiple launch sites because different orbital destinations require different launch geometries and azimuths. Cape Canaveral’s southeast-coast location provides access to low and medium inclination orbits used by communications satellites, Earth-observing satellites, and ISS supply missions, as well as to geostationary orbits and lunar or interplanetary trajectories. Kennedy Space Center’s LC-39A supports crewed Dragon launches and space-station resupply missions alongside commercial satellite launches. Vandenberg’s California-coast location provides access to high-inclination and polar orbits used by satellite communication constellations, defense intelligence satellites, Earth-observing satellites, and some lunar missions. Starbase is being built as a commercial launch site dedicated to orbital missions and to Starship development. Using several sites therefore allows SpaceX to match each mission to the launch location best suited for its required orbit and payload.
